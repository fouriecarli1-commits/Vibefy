## The three sign-in items, in full

Added 2026-09-23, after the sign-in surface was built out. Each is short in the
table above because the table is an index; this is the part you would need to
act on one.

### Signing in with somebody else's account

**Correcting what I told you first:** I said this needs the primary domain.
That is not right, and it matters because it made the item look blocked behind
another item when it is not. Google's redirect URI points at _Supabase_, not at
us, and that address exists today. What the domain affects is the consent
screen Google shows your users, not whether the thing works.

Three steps in consoles this repository cannot reach, and then one command:

1. **Create an OAuth app with the provider.** For Google that is Google Cloud
   Console → APIs & Services → Credentials → Create OAuth client ID → Web
   application. The authorised redirect URI is Supabase's, not ours, and it is
   the same one for every provider:
   `https://<your-project-ref>.supabase.co/auth/v1/callback`. The project ref is
   in the Supabase URL you already have. Copy the client id and the client
   secret.
2. **Supabase → Authentication → Providers → the provider → enable**, and paste
   both values.
3. **Supabase → Authentication → URL Configuration.** The Site URL and the
   Redirect URLs list must include wherever the app is actually deployed, with
   the path: `https://<wherever>/auth/callback`. This is the step that is easy
   to miss, because everything up to it succeeds and the failure arrives at the
   very end of the flow, after Google has already said yes.
4. **Run `pnpm providers`.** There is no flag to set and nothing to redeploy:
   the sign-in page asks Supabase which providers are on, cached for five
   minutes, so the button appears by itself. That command is also how to find
   out why it is not there — it prints which providers are enabled and, when
   Google is not, these steps with your project's redirect URI filled in.

   There was a flag, `NEXT_PUBLIC_GOOGLE_SIGN_IN=on`, and it was a mistake of
   exactly the kind this product exists to find. Absent-rather-than-broken is
   right for a visitor; for the person setting it up, a page that renders
   nothing is indistinguishable from a feature that was never built, and Anré
   asked twice where the Google sign-in was. Supabase already knows the answer,
   so it is the one that gets asked.

**Where the domain does come in.** Google's OAuth consent screen asks for an
application name, a support email, a homepage and links to a privacy policy and
terms. Those links are shown to every person who clicks the button, so they
should be our own pages on our own domain rather than a deployment URL — for
the same reason the badge licence forbids the mark pointing anywhere but the
verification page. Whether Google additionally requires domain verification
depends on which scopes are requested and on rules they change; we request only
the basic ones (email, profile, openid), which are not the sensitive category,
but check that on the day rather than trusting this paragraph.

**What it does not need.** No new secret in our repository, no migration, and no
change to the consent-recording path: the accepted document versions already
travel to the callback and are checked there against the registry we publish.

### Removing a second step

The lock-out is real and it is the one thing I would not build without saying
so. A password reset email lands you at the second step as well, so it does not
get anybody past a phone they no longer have. The panel says that before
anybody enrols and tells them to enrol two devices in the same sitting, which is
the difference between an inconvenience and losing the organisation. That is
mitigation, not a route.

**The code is small.** `SUPABASE_SERVICE_ROLE_KEY` is already an environment
variable this project expects, and Supabase exposes
`auth.admin.mfa.deleteFactor({ id, userId })`. An admin screen beside
`/admin/accounts`, a row per enrolled factor, a button, and an entry in the
audit trail. An afternoon.

**The policy is the item.** Who may ask, and what we require before we agree,
is the whole question — and getting it wrong is worse than having no route at
all, because a support process that removes a second factor on the strength of
a convincing email _is_ the way past the second factor. A rule has to survive
somebody who has read our website, knows the customer's name and application,
and is patient. What we cannot use as proof: knowing the email address, knowing
what the account owns, or sending mail from it — an address is what gets
compromised first, and it is the thing the second step exists to stop being
sufficient.

Things that could stand in its place, none of them decided:

- **A waiting period with notice.** We remove the factor seven days after the
  request and email the account throughout. Somebody who still holds the
  account sees the notice and stops it. This is what most providers do and it
  is the cheapest honest answer.
- **Proof against something recorded before the request.** A domain
  verification record on an application the account already owns, which is a
  control we already check for assessments and which an attacker does not have.
- **A second enrolled device**, which makes the whole question moot and is why
  the panel asks for one.

This also needs the contact email from the table above: a recovery route with
nowhere to write is not one.

### Requiring a second step

Today it is optional and that is deliberate. Requiring it of a solo builder
trying a free assessment costs us the customer and buys them nothing — their
account holds one unassessed application. The accounts where it starts to
matter hold something somebody else would want: the ability to issue a badge,
to invite a colleague into a workspace, or to read a client's findings.

**What "required" would actually mean.** Supabase's assurance level is a
property of a _session_, not of an account, and `decideSecondStep` already reads
it at sign-in. Requiring it is therefore not one switch. Three places would
have to decide, and each has a different failure if it is missed:

- **At sign-in**, refusing a session below the required level. Cheap, and the
  least useful on its own, because a session that predates the rule is already
  in somebody's browser.
- **At the action**, server-side, on the handful that matter — issuing, the
  invitation, the report. This is the one that is actually load-bearing, and it
  is the one that is easy to leave out and never notice, exactly as the sign-in
  enforcement was.
- **At enrolment time**, deciding what happens to accounts that already exist
  and already qualify. Locking them out of their own console until they enrol is
  a support incident; letting them carry on is a rule that applies to nobody
  who matters.

**The decision I need from you is the friction, not the mechanism.** An agency
that has just paid and wants to invite two colleagues, told at that moment that
everybody needs an authenticator app first, is a real cost at a real moment.
Whether that cost is worth paying is a judgement about who our customers are.
Say which of the three surfaces should require it — or that none should — and
the rest is ordinary work.
